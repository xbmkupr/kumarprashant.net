<p class="standfirst">Rollo May, the existential psychologist, believed that we frequently mistake opposites.</p>
<blockquote>“Hate is not the opposite of love; apathy is.”</blockquote>
<p>Hate is destructive, but it still contains attention, energy and emotional investment. Apathy is colder. It removes the other person from the field of concern altogether.</p>
<p>Amish Tripathi reaches a similar conclusion in <em>The Secret of the Nagas</em>: “Hate is just love gone bad.” The true absence of love begins when we no longer care what happens to another person.</p>
<p>This is not merely an observation about relationships. It says something important about what it means to live deliberately.</p>
<h2>We become through choosing</h2>
<p>An acorn becomes an oak through automatic growth. A kitten becomes a cat by instinct. Neither must decide what it will become or commit itself to the process.</p>
<p>Human beings are different. Biology may carry us into adulthood, but it cannot make us fully human. Character is formed through the many decisions we make, the commitments we accept and the responsibilities we continue to carry when convenience disappears.</p>
<p>Our dignity is not a finished quality handed to us at birth. It is built, decision by decision.</p>
<p>Choice sounds liberating, but every meaningful choice also closes alternatives. To choose one direction is to decline another. To commit is to place something of ourselves at risk. That is why decisions of consequence require courage.</p>
<h2>Commitment does not abolish doubt</h2>
<p>We sometimes imagine that genuine commitment should feel like permanent certainty. If doubt appears, we treat it as evidence that the commitment is weak or the decision was wrong.</p>
<p>May argued for something more mature:</p>
<blockquote>“Commitment is healthiest when it is not without doubt but in spite of doubt.”</blockquote>
<p>Doubt can coexist with conviction. In fact, a commitment that has never encountered doubt may simply be an untested preference.</p>
<p>Healthy commitment is neither blind persistence nor restless second-guessing. It is the willingness to examine our direction honestly and still decide where to place our weight.</p>
<h2>Independence makes love possible</h2>
<p>Love is often confused with dependence. Dependence says: I need you in order to remain whole. Love says: I can stand as myself and still choose to care deeply about what happens to you.</p>
<p>May’s point was not that people should become detached or entirely self-sufficient. It was that mature love grows in proportion to our capacity for independence. Without that inner standing, affection can quietly become possession, fear or control.</p>
<p>Independence therefore is not the enemy of commitment. It gives commitment meaning. A choice matters because we were capable of choosing otherwise.</p>
<h2>Direction before speed</h2>
<p>Care, choice and commitment all require attention. That attention becomes difficult when movement itself is mistaken for progress.</p>
<p>May offered a final warning:</p>
<blockquote>“It is an old and ironic habit of human beings to run faster when we have lost our way.”</blockquote>
<p>Speed can disguise uncertainty for a while. Activity can protect us from the discomfort of asking whether the direction still deserves our commitment. But moving faster does not turn the wrong road into the right one.</p>
<p>The harder discipline is to pause, recover our bearings and choose again.</p>
<h2>Final thought</h2>
<p>Apathy asks nothing of us. Care asks us to notice. Love asks us to remain independent enough to choose. Commitment asks us to continue despite doubt. Courage is what allows those choices to become a life.</p>
<p class="final-thought">We do not become ourselves automatically. We become ourselves by deciding what deserves our care—and then placing our weight behind it.</p>
